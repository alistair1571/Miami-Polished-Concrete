Can I have polished concrete in my Downtown or Edgewater condo?

Sometimes, but usually not by grinding the structural slab. Upper-floor units generally need association approval and a sound-rated underlayment under hard floors, and tower slabs are often post-tensioned, which limits how deep anyone can grind. The usual way to get the look is microcement over a sound-rated build-up. Some ground-floor units, lofts and buildings designed with polished concrete floors can be polished directly.

Why floors in towers are different

  • Sound rules. Florida building codes and most condo associations require hard floors to limit impact and airborne noise to the unit below. Associations set their own minimum IIC and STC ratings, often require a specific underlayment and submittals, and sometimes a sound test after installation.
  • Post-tensioned slabs. Most modern towers use slabs with steel tendons under high tension. Deep grinding, coring or drilling without scanning can hit a tendon. Contractors should keep grinding shallow and scan (for example with GPR) before any drilling.
  • Building logistics. Association approval, a certificate of insurance naming the association and management company, weekday working hours, freight elevator bookings, loading dock rules, deposits, hallway dust containment and rules on slurry in drains.
  • Noise travels. Grinding transmits through the structure, so buildings limit loud work to set hours.
  • Terraces are the exception. Upper-floor slabs are drier, which helps microcement. Terraces and balconies are exposed to rain and part of the building's waterproofing.

Just bought a new unit? Do the floors before you move in

Some new towers deliver finished floors; others hand over bare concrete in some or all rooms. Either way, the easiest time to change floors is before furniture arrives: approval paperwork can run alongside closing, walls and cabinets are protected once, and microcement can continue up into bathrooms, showers and backsplashes. Other Downtown and Edgewater buildings

  • Lofts and boutique buildings: some have exposed concrete designed to be polished. Re-polishing or restoring these is often straightforward.
  • Older low-rise buildings in Edgewater and Park West: may have terrazzo under later flooring, or slabs on grade that can be polished. Test old adhesives for asbestos first.
  • Restaurants and bars: polished concrete or microcement front of house, urethane cement in kitchens, night work coordinated with building management.
  • Retail, galleries and showrooms: high-gloss polish or microcement in the Arts & Entertainment District and along Biscayne Boulevard.
  • Office conversions: carpet and vinyl removed, then polish or microcement. Expect old wall lines and adhesive residue.
  • Historic Downtown buildings: original terrazzo or tile may be worth restoring; designated buildings may need preservation review.

Balconies and terraces

Microcement and exterior microtoppings can refresh balcony and terrace floors, but balconies are usually part of the building's structure and waterproofing. Get association approval first, and never grind or drill balcony slabs without it. Where there's concrete damage or rust staining, the association's engineer should look at it before anything cosmetic is done.

Common questions

Can I polish the concrete floor in my high-rise condo?

Usually not directly. Most associations require sound-rated underlayment under hard floors, and tower slabs are often post-tensioned. Microcement over a sound-rated build-up gives the look and is designed to meet the rules.

What is a post-tensioned slab and why does it matter?

It's a concrete slab with steel tendons under tension. Deep grinding, coring or drilling can damage them, so contractors must work shallow and scan before drilling.

What sound rating does my building require?

It varies. Your association's rules or renovation packet will state the minimum IIC and STC ratings and any approved underlayments.

Can microcement go over my existing tile?

Often, if the tile is well bonded and the sound-rated layer beneath it meets the building's rules. Otherwise the tile comes up and a new build-up goes down.

How long does association approval take?

Typically one to four weeks, depending on the building.

Can I redo my balcony floor?

Only with association approval. Balconies are often part of the building's structure and waterproofing.

Do you work in restaurants and retail in Downtown?

Yes, including tenant spaces in towers, usually overnight and coordinated with building management.

Is it better to do floors before moving in?

Yes. Approval can run alongside closing, and the work is faster, cleaner and cheaper without furniture.
