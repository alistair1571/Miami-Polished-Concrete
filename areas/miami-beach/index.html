Check for terrazzo before anyone grinds

Terrazzo took off in Miami Beach in the Art Deco era of the 1920s and 1930s and stayed popular through the mid-century MiMo years. Many homes, apartments and hotels from that period were built with terrazzo floors that later disappeared under carpet or tile.

If you lift a corner of old flooring and see chips of marble or glass set in a cement or resin matrix, it's terrazzo, not plain concrete. It needs restoration, not the process used on a structural slab:

  • Grinding out old patches and lippage between sections
  • Filling cracks, chips and carpet-tack holes, which are common in vintage terrazzo, with color-matched filler
  • Honing and polishing, then sealing
  • Replacing damaged sections with matched mix where needed

It's usually worth saving, and it's a specialist trade. Tell whoever quotes what you've found before any work starts. Terrazzo restoration guide. [Partner: do they restore terrazzo, or refer to a specialist?]

Miami Beach houses

Single-family homes on the island, from mid-century houses in North Beach and Normandy Isles to newer builds, are usually on slabs and suit polished concrete well. In older homes, check three things before any grinding:

  • Terrazzo under carpet or tile (see above).
  • Black adhesive under old vinyl or tile in homes built or renovated before the mid-1980s. Have it lab-tested for asbestos before anyone grinds. Why this matters
  • Moisture coming up through ground-floor slabs, especially in low-lying streets. A moisture test comes before any dye or color.

Condos: sound rules and the city's flooring permit

Most Miami Beach homes are condos, and the city is stricter than most about hard floors.

  • The code. Florida's building code requires floors between dwelling units to reach IIC 50 and STC 50. A bare concrete slab rates far lower, and a polished slab can't have an acoustic mat under it.
  • The city. The City of Miami Beach requires a flooring permit for condominium units, with a flooring permit affidavit and the soundproofing manufacturer's literature showing a code-compliant assembly. A soundproofing inspection is mandatory.
  • Plan review. Balconies and terraces, bathrooms, lobbies and other public areas get trade plan review; interior unit flooring is simpler, but the soundproofing inspection still applies.
  • Your association may set a higher bar, such as IIC 55 or more, and needs to approve before the city will.

In practice: in an upper-floor Miami Beach unit, a bare polished slab usually won't pass. Ground-floor units with no neighbor below, lobbies over non-residential space, and commercial units are where polishing is realistic. Hotels, restaurants and retail

Miami Beach's hotels, restaurants and shops put floors through sand, spilled drinks, luggage wheels and constant foot traffic. Polished concrete suits the island's modern interiors and is easy to keep clean.

  • Hotel lobbies and corridors: Usual finish: Semi-gloss, salt and pepper; Notes: Overnight phasing; check what's below upper-floor areas
  • Restaurants and bars: Usual finish: Satin with anti-slip treatment; Notes: Grippier at entrances, bars and service stations
  • Retail and galleries: Usual finish: Semi-gloss to high gloss; Notes: Reflects lighting; hides less dust than satin
  • Back-of-house and service corridors: Usual finish: Matte; Notes: Durable, low cost; kitchens are better with urethane cement
  • Pool decks and terraces: Usual finish: Not polished; Notes: Wet polished concrete is slick; use a textured or sealed finish

Flooding, salt air and sand

  • Flooding. Much of Miami Beach sits only a few feet above sea level, and low-lying streets flood during king tides and heavy rain. A polished slab isn't ruined by water the way wood, laminate and vinyl are; it's cleaned, dried and re-burnished if needed.
  • Moisture from below. Ground-floor slabs can see moisture pushing up, especially in the rainy season. Polished concrete tolerates it; dyes and topical sealers are more sensitive, so test first.
  • White haze. Efflorescence (natural salts carried up by moisture) can appear on ground-floor slabs. It isn't structural damage and can be cleaned off.
  • Sand. Sand is abrasive and dulls any polished floor. Walk-off mats, daily dust-mopping and a periodic burnish keep the shine.

Common questions

Can I polish the concrete floor in my Miami Beach condo?

In a unit with another unit below, usually not as a bare polished slab. Florida's building code requires sound-rated floors between units, and the City of Miami Beach requires a flooring permit with soundproofing documentation and a mandatory soundproofing inspection. Ground-floor units and houses are different.

I found terrazzo under my carpet. Should I polish it?

Don't treat it like plain concrete. Terrazzo needs restoration by a terrazzo specialist: grinding out old repairs, filling cracks and tack holes, then honing and polishing. It's usually worth saving.

Can you polish floors in a Miami Beach house?

Yes. Single-family homes on slabs are well suited. In homes built before the mid-1980s, test any black adhesive for asbestos before grinding, and check for terrazzo under old flooring.

Will flooding damage a polished concrete floor?

Polished concrete isn't ruined by water the way wood, laminate and vinyl often are. After a flood it's cleaned, dried and, if needed, re-burnished. Dyes and topical sealers are more vulnerable.

Do beach sand and salt damage polished concrete?

Sand is abrasive and dulls any polished floor over time. Walk-off mats at entrances, regular dust-mopping and a periodic burnish keep the shine.

Can you polish hotel or restaurant floors without closing?

Yes. Work is usually phased and done overnight, with vacuum dust extraction, so guests and diners aren't disturbed.

Which parts of Miami Beach do you cover?

All of Miami Beach, including South Beach, Mid-Beach and North Beach, plus Surfside, Bal Harbour, Bay Harbor Islands and North Bay Village.
