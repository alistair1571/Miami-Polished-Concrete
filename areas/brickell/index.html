Condo units: the sound rules come first

Before thinking about sheen or aggregate, check whether a polished floor is allowed in your unit at all.

  • The code. Section 1207 of the Florida Building Code requires floor/ceiling assemblies between dwelling units to reach IIC 50 (impact noise: footsteps, dropped items) and STC 50 (airborne noise: voices, TV).
  • Your association. Many Miami associations set a higher bar, such as IIC 55 or 60, and may require a notarized affidavit, board pre-approval or a specific tested assembly.
  • The slab. A bare concrete slab rates only around IIC 28 to 35. Tile, wood and vinyl reach the target with an acoustic underlayment beneath them, but a polished slab is the floor, so there's nowhere to put one.
  • The exemption. Units with no dwelling below them, such as ground-floor units, aren't covered by the between-units requirement. Your association's own rules may still apply.

In short: in most upper-floor Brickell units, a bare polished slab won't meet the rules. Polishing is realistic in ground-floor units, lobbies, amenity spaces and commercial units. Always check with your association and the City of Miami building department before starting, and don't pull up carpet or tile until the new floor is approved.

Sources: Florida Building Code §1207 as summarized by Commercial Acoustics (June 2026); slab IIC range from National Concrete Polishing (Feb 2026). This is general guidance, not a code determination for your building.

Your options, by type of space

  • Upper-floor condo unit (unit below): Polished concrete?: Usually not as a bare slab; Notes: Consider microcement or another concrete-look finish over a tested sound-rated build-up
  • Ground-floor unit (no unit below): Polished concrete?: Often yes; Notes: Association approval, COI and working hours still apply
  • Lobby, mail room, corridors: Polished concrete?: Yes, if no units directly below; Notes: Overnight phasing keeps access open
  • Gym, amenity rooms, party rooms: Polished concrete?: Check what's below; Notes: Often over parking or service areas
  • Retail, restaurants, offices: Polished concrete?: Yes; Notes: Commercial units; landlord or association approval
  • Balconies and terraces: Polished concrete?: Rarely polished; Notes: Waterproofing and slip risk; see below

For a concrete look in an upper-floor unit, ask for the tested IIC and STC rating of the full assembly, meaning your slab, the underlayment and the finish together, and submit that to the association. The rating belongs to the assembly, not to one product.

What Brickell associations and managers ask for

  • A written application describing the work, the finish and the schedule.
  • A certificate of insurance naming the association, at the limits it specifies.
  • The contractor's Florida licence and, often, workers' compensation proof.
  • A method statement for dust and noise: planetary grinders with vacuum extraction and HEPA filtration.
  • Product data sheets for densifiers, guards and fillers.
  • Sound documentation for any hard floor in a unit with neighbors below.
  • A service elevator booking, floor and elevator protection, and work only within permitted hours.

Grinding is loud and carries through a tower's structure, which is why hours are strictly enforced. Sending the paperwork early is the fastest way to get approved.

Post-tensioned slabs: why grind depth matters

Many Brickell towers are built with post-tensioned concrete slabs, with steel tendons running through them under very high tension. Polishing removes only a thin surface layer, so it's normally compatible. But deep grinding, core drilling, saw-cutting or anchoring into a post-tensioned slab can hit a tendon, which is dangerous and expensive.

  • Keep to a cream or salt-and-pepper finish; don't chase deep exposed aggregate.
  • No cutting, coring or new anchors without scanning and sign-off from the building's engineer.
  • Ask any contractor quoting a tower job how they handle post-tensioned slabs. If they don't know the term, keep looking.

Lobbies, corridors and amenity spaces

Common areas are where polished concrete makes most sense in a Brickell tower. Polished lobby floors have been used in Brickell buildings for years, typically at a medium gloss that reflects the lighting without looking wet.

  • Overnight phasing so residents always have a way through.
  • Satin rather than high gloss at the entrance, where rain comes in on shoes most summer afternoons; add walk-off matting.
  • Check what's below amenity rooms. Over parking or service areas, polishing is usually straightforward.
  • A written maintenance plan for the building's janitorial staff.

Retail, restaurants and offices

Brickell's shops, restaurants and office floors, around Brickell City Centre, Mary Brickell Village and along Brickell Avenue, suit polished concrete well: it's durable, quick to clean and fits the neighborhood's modern interiors.

  • Retail and showrooms: semi-gloss to high gloss to reflect the lighting.
  • Restaurants and bars: satin with an anti-slip treatment near entrances and drink stations; handles nightly mopping.
  • Offices: satin salt-and-pepper, which hides scuffs.
  • Overnight work so you open as normal; landlord or association approval as for residential units.

Balconies and terraces

Some new Brickell towers are marketing polished concrete balconies. For an existing balcony, be careful:

  • Balconies are usually controlled by the association, and you'll need approval for any change to the surface.
  • Many carry a waterproofing system that grinding would damage.
  • Wet polished concrete is slippery, and balconies get wet. A textured or sealed finish is safer than a high polish.

Common questions

Can I have polished concrete floors in my Brickell condo?

In a ground-floor unit with no neighbor below, often yes, with association approval. In upper-floor units, usually not as a bare polished slab, because Florida's building code requires sound-rated floors between units (IIC 50 and STC 50) and associations often require more.

Why can't a polished slab meet the sound rules?

A bare concrete slab rates far below IIC 50 for impact noise. Other hard floors reach the rating with an acoustic underlayment, but a polished slab is the floor itself, so there's nowhere to put one.

How can I get a concrete look in an upper-floor unit?

The usual route is a concrete-effect finish such as microcement, installed over an acoustic underlayment that's tested as a full assembly. Get the tested rating in writing and submit it to your association.

Is it safe to grind a post-tensioned slab?

Surface grinding for polishing removes only a thin layer and is routinely done on post-tensioned slabs. Deep grinding, cutting or drilling should only happen after scanning and sign-off, which is why tower floors usually stay at a cream or salt-and-pepper finish.

Can you polish our building lobby or amenity spaces?

Yes. Lobbies, corridors on levels without units below, gyms and amenity decks are common polishing projects. Work can run overnight to keep the lobby open.

Can I polish my balcony?

Balconies are usually controlled by the association and carry a waterproofing system, and wet polished concrete is slippery. Get association approval, and choose a textured or sealed finish rather than a high polish.

What will my association ask for?

Typically a written application, a certificate of insurance naming the association, the contractor's licence, a method statement for dust and noise, product data sheets, an elevator booking and working within set hours.
