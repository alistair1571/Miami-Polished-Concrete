Older Medley slabs

Medley was incorporated in 1949, and much of its industrial stock dates from the 1970s and 1980s. Those slabs usually polish well, but they come with history:

  • Broken joint edges from decades of forklift and pallet-jack traffic. They're rebuilt and filled with semi-rigid polyurea before polishing.
  • Spalls, patches and trenches from past tenants' equipment and plumbing. Repaired first; patches will show as a different tone.
  • Old paint, striping and failed coatings to grind off.
  • Slabs that move under loaded trucks need stabilizing before any joint filling, or the filler fails.
  • Uneven floors: grinding shows more stone on high spots than low ones. A test panel shows the likely result.

Medley sits beside the quarries and cement plants that supply much of Miami's concrete, so many of these slabs are made from local limestone aggregate, which gives a pale, speckled look when polished.

Rail docks, truck docks and wet doorways

The Florida East Coast Railway's freight line serves many Medley businesses, and most units have truck docks or grade-level doors. Floors near them take the most punishment and get wet in summer storms.

  • Phased dock work so loading carries on.
  • Matte finish and matting in the first bays inside each door, where rain and grit come in.
  • Dock plate and threshold edges checked and repaired before polishing.
  • Anti-slip treatment where floors regularly get wet.

Dust in Medley buildings

There are two kinds of dust in a Medley warehouse. The first comes from the slab itself: unsealed concrete slowly wears and powders under traffic. Polishing and densifying stops that. The second blows in from trucks, yards and neighboring industrial operations, and no floor stops that on its own.

  • A polished floor is far easier to sweep and scrub, so tracked-in dust comes up instead of grinding in.
  • Walk-off and dock matting catches grit at the doors.
  • Regular auto-scrubbing with a pH-neutral cleaner keeps the sheen. Maintenance program

Landlords and portfolio owners

Some owners in Medley hold dozens or hundreds of warehouse units. For them, floors are a leasing tool:

  • One specification across the portfolio (for example matte cream with filled joints) keeps units consistent and simple to quote.
  • Work between tenants, when the unit is empty and there's no racking.
  • Agreed unit pricing for repeat work lowers the cost per unit.
  • A polished floor shows well on tours, especially in older buildings competing with newer stock.

Common questions

How much does warehouse floor polishing cost in Medley?

Large Medley warehouse floors usually price at the low end of Miami's $3 to $8 per sq ft range for matte or satin. Older slabs often need joint repair, spall repair and coating removal, which are itemized separately.

Our building is from the 1970s or 80s. Can the slab still be polished?

Usually. Older slabs often have worn joints, patches and uneven areas, so expect more repair work and some variation in how the aggregate shows. A test panel on your floor shows the likely result.

Will polishing stop the dust in our warehouse?

It stops the slab itself from dusting and makes the floor much easier to sweep and scrub. In Medley, a lot of dust also blows in from trucks and nearby industry, so matting and a cleaning routine still matter.

Can you work around rail docks and truck traffic?

Yes. Dock areas and aisles are phased so loading continues, and the first bays inside doors are usually kept matte for grip.

Is polished concrete suitable for a truck repair shop or fuel business?

For offices, parts storage and aisles, yes. Service bays where oils, fuels and solvents are handled are usually better protected with a chemical-resistant coating.

We own several warehouses in Medley. Can you set up one spec for all of them?

Yes. A standard specification and price for units between tenants keeps the look consistent across a portfolio and lowers the cost per unit.
