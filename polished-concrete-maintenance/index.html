Polished Concrete Maintenance & Restoration in Miami

Polished concrete is low-maintenance, not no-maintenance. Sand, spills, the wrong cleaner and heavy traffic dull it over time, and in most cases it can be brought back without starting again.

Is your floor polished, or coated?

Many floors sold as "polished concrete" are actually ground and then sealed with a topical coating. They look similar when new, but they wear and are fixed completely differently.

  • Traffic lanes: True polished concrete: Dull evenly; no film; Sealed or coated floor: Scuffs, whitens, peels or flakes
  • Edges and doorways: True polished concrete: Same as the rest of the floor; Sealed or coated floor: Coating may lift at edges
  • Scratches: True polished concrete: Fine scratches in the concrete; Sealed or coated floor: Scratches in a film on top
  • After a spill: True polished concrete: May etch (dull ring); Sealed or coated floor: Often whitens or clouds the coating
  • Fix: True polished concrete: Burnish, re-guard or re-polish; Sealed or coated floor: Strip or grind the coating off, then polish or recoat

If you're not sure, a restorer can tell in minutes on site. Knowing which you have avoids paying for the wrong fix.

Restoration levels, from quickest to most involved

  • 1. Deep clean: What's done: Scrub with the right cleaner and pad; neutralize residue; Fixes: Film, dirt, cleaner buildup; Rough cost: Low
  • 2. Burnish: What's done: High-speed burnish with diamond-impregnated pads; Fixes: Light dulling in traffic lanes; Rough cost: Low; often part of a maintenance plan
  • 3. Re-guard: What's done: Apply and burnish a new stain guard or densifier; Fixes: Lost stain resistance, sheen; Rough cost: About $0.50–$1.00 per sq ft
  • 4. Hone and re-polish: What's done: Fine resin diamonds through the upper grits; Fixes: Worn lanes, light scratches, etching; Rough cost: About $1–$3 per sq ft
  • 5. Regrind and polish: What's done: Coarser diamonds, then the full sequence; Fixes: Deep scratches, heavy etching, failed coatings; Rough cost: Approaching a new job

Repairs such as crack routing and filling, joint filler replacement and spall patching are priced separately, usually per linear foot or per repair. Dyed floors: levels 4–5 can lighten or remove dye; mention it before work starts.

Stains and etching

Stains and etching look similar but are different problems. A stain is something that soaked in and darkened the floor. Etching is the acid in a spill dissolving the polished surface, leaving a dull, lighter mark. Cleaners can lift some stains, but they can't fix etching.

  • Etching (dull rings): Common Miami causes: Lime and citrus, cocktails, wine, soda, vinegar cleaners, some tile and grout cleaners; What to do: Professional spot hone and polish, then re-guard
  • Oil and grease: Common Miami causes: Kitchens, garages, cars, restaurants; What to do: Degreaser or poultice; hone if it persists
  • Coffee, wine, food: Common Miami causes: Homes, cafés, restaurants; What to do: Clean promptly; poultice if set
  • Rust: Common Miami causes: Metal furniture, planters, tools; What to do: Rust remover made for concrete, carefully; hone if deep
  • Tire marks: Common Miami causes: Garages and showrooms; What to do: Degreaser and scrub
  • Paint and adhesive: Common Miami causes: Renovations; What to do: Careful scraping and solvent; hone if needed

Prevention: wipe acidic spills straight away, keep the guard maintained, and never use vinegar or citrus cleaners on polished concrete.

Cleaning schedules by type of space

  • Home: Daily: Dust-mop high-traffic areas; Weekly: Damp-mop with pH-neutral cleaner; Periodic: Re-guard every few years; burnish traffic paths as needed
  • Restaurant or bar: Daily: Sweep and auto-scrub or mop with neutral cleaner; wipe spills fast; Weekly: Detail-clean bar and service areas; Periodic: Burnish and re-guard more often; spot-repair etching
  • Retail or showroom: Daily: Dust-mop; auto-scrub with neutral cleaner; Weekly: Burnish if gloss matters; Periodic: Re-guard every 1–3 years
  • Warehouse: Daily: Sweep or auto-scrub main aisles; Weekly: Scrub docks and doorways; Periodic: Burnish main routes; inspect joints
  • Office or clinic: Daily: Dust-mop; Weekly: Neutral scrub; Periodic: Re-guard every few years

What not to use

  • Vinegar, citrus or acidic cleaners. They etch the surface.
  • Bleach and ammonia in regular use.
  • Shine or "mop and glow" cleaners and oil soaps, which leave a film that dulls and gets sticky.
  • Too much water. Flooding the floor can break down joint filler over time.
  • Aggressive pads not made for polished concrete.

Miami problems, and their fixes

  • Sand. Beach and yard sand is abrasive and the main cause of dull lanes. Walk-off mats at every entrance, and daily dust-mopping near the beach.
  • Lime, citrus and cocktails. Miami bars and restaurants see more acid etching than most. A strong guard, fast wiping and scheduled spot repairs keep it under control.
  • Efflorescence. White haze on ground-floor slabs comes from moisture carrying salts up. Clean it dry, don't seal over it, and check moisture if it keeps coming back.
  • Flooding and storms. After water gets in, remove mud promptly, clean with a neutral cleaner, let the slab dry fully, then burnish and re-guard if needed. The floor itself usually survives; don't let silt dry on it.
  • Seasonal properties. Homes and shops closed for the summer do fine on polished concrete, but a clean and re-guard before reopening brings them back quickly.
  • Humidity. Guards and cleaners dry slower. Follow product drying times before traffic.

Fixing a floor that wasn't polished properly

If a floor looked great when it was done and went dull within months, the original job probably cut a corner:

  • No densifier or guard, so the surface wears and stains quickly.
  • Stopped at a low grit, so the "shine" was mostly the guard.
  • Grind-and-seal sold as polished, with a topical sealer that scuffs and peels.
  • Rushed passes, leaving swirl marks that show in low sun.

Most of these can be corrected by re-polishing through the grit sequence, densifying and guarding, often for less than the original job. If there's a coating on top, it comes off first. Polished or coated?

Maintenance plans for businesses

For restaurants, shops, showrooms, offices and warehouses, a scheduled plan costs less than letting a floor wear down and restoring it.

  • A site walk to set the schedule by traffic and use.
  • Periodic burnishing of traffic lanes, often quarterly in busy retail and hospitality.
  • Guard reapplication on a set cycle.
  • Spot repairs of etching, stains and joints as they appear.
  • Training and a written cleaning spec for your own staff or janitorial contractor.

Common questions

Why has my polished concrete floor gone dull?

Usually one of four things: grit wearing the traffic lanes, a film of cleaner residue, acid etching from spills, or a floor that was never properly densified and guarded. Each has a different fix, from a deep clean to a light re-polish.

How much does it cost to restore a polished concrete floor?

Published figures put guard or densifier reapplication at about $0.50 to $1.00 per sq ft and a light re-polish at about $1 to $3 per sq ft. Deep scratches, coating removal or a full regrind cost more; small areas often carry a minimum charge.

How often should polished concrete be maintained?

Dust-mop daily or as needed, damp-mop or auto-scrub with a pH-neutral cleaner, reapply the guard every one to five years depending on traffic, and light re-polish high-traffic areas every several years.

How do I remove lime or wine marks from polished concrete?

Dull rings from acidic spills are etching, not stains, so cleaners won't remove them. They're honed out with fine diamond pads and re-guarded. Wipe acidic spills quickly to prevent them.

What cleaner should I use on polished concrete?

A pH-neutral cleaner made for polished concrete or stone. Avoid vinegar, citrus, bleach, ammonia and cleaners that leave a shine film, which build up and dull the floor.

Is my floor polished concrete or just sealed?

Look closely at traffic lanes and edges. If there's peeling, flaking, white scuffing or a visible film, it's probably a topical sealer or coating. True polished concrete has no film to peel, so the fix is different.

Can a floor that was polished badly be fixed?

Usually. If it dulled within months, it may have skipped densifier or guard, stopped at a low grit, or been a grind-and-seal. It can often be re-polished properly without starting from scratch.

Will restoration remove the color from my dyed floor?

Light burnishing and re-guarding won't. Aggressive honing with coarse grits can lighten or remove dye, so the floor may need re-dyeing. Tell the contractor if your floor is dyed.
