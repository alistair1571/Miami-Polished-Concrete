Microcement Floors in Miami

Microcement is a hand-troweled cement coating, just 2 to 3 mm thick, that gives a seamless concrete look over concrete, tile, walls and showers. In Miami it's often the answer when polished concrete isn't possible, especially in condos and over existing tile.

This page covers when to choose it over polished concrete, what Florida's condo sound rules mean for it, what goes wrong and why, and what it costs.

Microcement or polished concrete?

They look similar and get confused all the time, but they're different things. Polished concrete is your existing slab, ground and polished; nothing is added. Microcement is a thin decorative layer applied on top and protected by a sealer.

  • What it is: Polished concrete: The existing slab, ground, densified and polished; Microcement: A 2–3 mm cement coating, troweled on and sealed
  • Needs a concrete slab?: Polished concrete: Yes, in good condition; Microcement: No: goes over concrete, tile, plaster and more
  • Over existing tile: Polished concrete: Tile must come up first; Microcement: Can go over firmly bonded tile
  • Walls, showers, stairs, countertops: Polished concrete: No; Microcement: Yes
  • Look: Polished concrete: Natural, speckled; each slab different; Microcement: Uniform color of your choice; hand-troweled texture
  • Wearing surface: Polished concrete: The hardened concrete itself; Microcement: The sealer, which needs periodic maintenance
  • Condo units with a unit below: Polished concrete: Usually ruled out by sound rules; Microcement: Possible over a sound-rated build-up
  • Typical cost: Polished concrete: $3–$12 per sq ft; Microcement: About $8–$34 per sq ft

Choose polished concrete if you have a sound slab, want the most durable and lowest-maintenance floor, and don't have a neighbor below. Polished concrete for homes

Choose microcement if you want to keep existing tile in place, need the look on walls or in a bathroom, want a specific uniform color, or live in an upper-floor condo where polishing isn't allowed.

Microcement in Miami condos: what the sound rules mean

Many people come to microcement because they've learned they can't polish the slab in their condo. It can work, but it isn't a sound solution by itself.

  • The rule. Florida's building code requires floors between dwelling units to reach IIC 50 (impact noise) and STC 50 (airborne noise), and many associations require more. A bare slab rates far below that.
  • Microcement doesn't change that. A 2–3 mm coating applied straight to the slab adds essentially nothing to impact-noise performance.
  • It can't go straight onto a soft acoustic mat either. A thin rigid coating over a soft, springy layer will crack. Sound-rated floors that take a hard finish use a resilient mat under a rigid layer, such as a poured underlayment or board, with the finish on top.
  • That build-up adds height. Expect changes at doors, thresholds and transitions to other rooms, and plan for it.
  • The rating belongs to the whole assembly. Ask for the tested IIC and STC of the full system (your slab, the mat, the rigid layer and the finish) and submit it to the association. In Miami Beach, the city also requires soundproofing documentation with the flooring permit and a soundproofing inspection.

Before you commit: get your association's written flooring rules, choose a system with a published tested assembly that meets them, and get approval before anything is removed. [Partner/installer: name the sound-rated system you use under microcement, with its tested ratings]

Microcement over existing tile

Going over tile is one of microcement's biggest advantages: no demolition, no dust from breaking up tile, no debris. It only works if the tile underneath is sound.

  • Hollow or loose tiles: What goes wrong: They flex under load and crack the coating above; How it's prevented: Tap-test every tile; fix or remove hollow ones first
  • Grout lines: What goes wrong: Faint lines "ghost" through months later; How it's prevented: Fill and level every joint before the first coat
  • Glazed porcelain: What goes wrong: Poor bonding and peeling; How it's prevented: A bonding primer made for non-porous surfaces
  • Rushed coats: What goes wrong: Weak layers, blotches, early wear; How it's prevented: Respect drying times between coats, which are longer in Miami humidity

These are preparation failures, not flaws in the material. They're also the most common reasons microcement jobs are redone.

Bathrooms and showers

Seamless walls and floors with no grout to clean are a big part of microcement's appeal in bathrooms. But advice on waterproofing varies: some suppliers say their system is waterproof on its own, while other installers warn that skipping a waterproofing membrane in shower zones is a common cause of failure.

Our advice: in showers and wet zones, insist on the manufacturer's full waterproofing specification, in writing, and don't rely on the sealer alone. Add an anti-slip finish to shower floors.

Cracks and joints: the honest version

You'll read that microcement "doesn't crack" and "doesn't need joints". That's overselling it.

  • It follows what's underneath. A moving crack or joint in the slab can show through as a crack in the coating.
  • Structural and control joints in the slab should be respected, not simply troweled over.
  • Hairline cracks in the slab can usually be treated and bridged during preparation.
  • Good preparation (sound substrate, proper primers, reinforcing mesh where the system calls for it) prevents most problems.

Miami conditions

  • Moisture from below. Ground-floor slabs can push moisture up. Unlike polished concrete, microcement is a sealed coating system, so test the slab first and use the manufacturer's moisture-tolerant primer where needed.
  • Humidity and drying time. Each coat needs to dry before the next. Miami humidity slows this down, and rushing it is a common cause of problems.
  • Outdoors. Balconies, terraces and pool surrounds need UV-stable sealers and an anti-slip finish. In condos, balconies are usually association-controlled and may have waterproofing that must be protected.
  • Sand. Grit wears the sealer. Walk-off mats and regular dust-mopping extend the time between reseals.

2

Repair, fill grout lines, prime (and add mesh where the system calls for it).

3

Troweled on in thin layers, each dried and sanded.

4

Finish coats

Finer layers in the chosen color and texture.

5

Seal

Several coats of sealer in matte, satin or gloss; anti-slip where needed.

Caring for microcement

The sealer is what protects microcement, so care is about looking after the sealer.

  • Dust-mop regularly; grit is abrasive.
  • Mop with a pH-neutral cleaner. Avoid bleach, ammonia, vinegar and abrasive pads.
  • Felt pads under furniture; don't drag heavy items.
  • Reseal periodically, more often in kitchens, entrances and busy areas. Resealing is a maintenance coat, not a tear-out. [Installer: typical reseal interval and cost]

Choosing a microcement installer

Microcement is hand-applied, so the result depends heavily on the installer. Ask:

  • Which system and brand will you use, and are you trained or certified by the manufacturer?
  • Can I see finished floors that are at least a year old, not just fresh photos?
  • How will you deal with my tile or slab: hollow tiles, grout lines, cracks, joints and moisture?
  • For a condo: which sound-rated assembly will sit underneath, and what are its tested IIC and STC ratings?
  • For showers: what's the waterproofing specification?
  • How many coats, what drying time between them, and which sealer?
  • What does the warranty cover, and what maintenance does it require?

Common questions

What is microcement?

A polymer-modified cement coating troweled on by hand in thin layers, usually 2 to 3 mm in total, then sealed. It gives a seamless, concrete-look finish over concrete, tile, plaster and other stable surfaces.

Is microcement the same as polished concrete?

No. Polished concrete is your existing slab ground and polished; nothing is added. Microcement is a thin decorative layer applied on top, protected by a sealer. It can go where polishing can't, such as over tile, on walls and in showers.

Can I put microcement in my upper-floor Miami condo?

Often, but not straight onto the slab if there's a unit below. Microcement is only a few millimeters thick and does nothing for impact noise on its own. To meet Florida's IIC 50 and STC 50 rules and your association's requirements, it usually needs to sit on a sound-rated floor build-up, and you need the tested rating for the full assembly.

Can microcement go over my existing tile?

Yes, if the tiles are firmly bonded. Hollow or loose tiles must be fixed or removed first, and grout lines must be filled and leveled, or they can show through as faint lines later.

Does microcement crack?

It can. It follows the surface underneath, so structural joints, moving cracks and hollow tiles can show through. Good preparation prevents most problems; honest installers won't promise it can never crack.

How much does microcement cost in Miami?

Published US prices mostly run from about $8 to $34 per sq ft installed, with high-end work and stairs going higher. It's usually more per sq ft than polished concrete, but it can save on demolition when it goes over existing tile.

How do I look after a microcement floor?

Sweep and mop with a pH-neutral cleaner, and avoid harsh or acidic products. The sealer is the wearing surface, so it needs a maintenance coat from time to time, more often in busy areas.

Can microcement be used outdoors or in showers?

Yes, with the right system: UV-stable sealers and an anti-slip finish outside, and a manufacturer-approved waterproofing approach in showers. Ask the installer exactly which products they'll use.
