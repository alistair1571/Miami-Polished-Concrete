Terrazzo Restoration in Miami

Thousands of Miami homes, apartments and hotels were built with terrazzo floors that later disappeared under carpet, vinyl or tile. If you've found terrazzo, it's usually worth saving.

This guide covers whether to restore, repair or replace, how the different polishing methods compare, what to check before anyone grinds, and what restoration costs.

Is it terrazzo?

Terrazzo is made of chips (usually marble, sometimes glass or granite) set in a binder and ground smooth. Lift a corner of carpet or a loose tile and look for:

  • Visible chips of stone or glass scattered through a cement-colored or colored background.
  • Metal or plastic divider strips forming a grid or pattern.
  • A continuous floor rather than individual tiles (though terrazzo tiles also exist).

Cement terrazzo is typical of older Miami floors. Terrazzo poured across Miami-Dade and Broward from the 1940s through the 1970s used local marble chips in a Portland cement binder set directly on the slab. The cement is porous and wears differently in a humid climate. Epoxy terrazzo is thinner and more common in newer commercial buildings; it's restored differently and is less porous.

Restore, repair or replace?

  • Dull, scratched or stained but intact: Restore: clean, hone, polish, seal
  • Mostly sound with holes, chips or a few cracks: Repair the damage, then restore
  • Old coating, wax or adhesive on top: Strip or grind it off, then restore
  • Large sections missing (removed walls, big plumbing cuts): Patch with matched mix, accepting some visible difference, or cover that area
  • Badly broken, heaving or mostly missing: Rebuild or replace; consider polished concrete or new terrazzo
  • You want the look but have no terrazzo: Polished concrete with exposed aggregate gives a similar speckled look for less

If you're tempted to tile or microcement over original terrazzo, get a restoration quote first. Covering it is easy to reverse on paper but hard in practice, and original terrazzo is part of what makes older Miami homes distinctive.

Before anyone grinds

Asbestos under old vinyl tile

Carpet laid over terrazzo usually leaves only tack strips and pad residue. Vinyl tile is different: in homes built or renovated before the mid-1980s, it was often stuck down with black mastic, which frequently contains asbestos. Grinding it spreads the fibers through the house.

If you find black or dark adhesive, stop, and have the tile and adhesive lab-tested separately. If it's positive, a licensed abatement contractor removes it before restoration starts. More on asbestos testing

Other things to check

  • Moisture coming up through ground-floor slabs, especially in low-lying areas. Penetrating sealers are chosen accordingly.
  • Cracks that move. Cracks reflecting slab movement can reopen after filling.
  • What's under built-in cabinets and closets. There may be gaps in the terrazzo where they were installed.

How terrazzo is restored

1

Strip

Remove carpet pad residue, adhesive, wax and old coatings.

2

Repair

Fill tack holes, chips and cracks with color-matched material; patch larger areas with matched mix.

3

Grind

Flatten lippage and uneven areas with metal-bond diamonds.

4

Hone and polish

Resin diamonds refine the surface to matte, satin or high gloss.

5

Seal

A penetrating sealer protects the cement binder from stains.

A typical home often takes three to seven working days, depending on adhesive, repairs and size.

Polishing methods compared

Quotes in the same price range can describe very different work. Ask which method is being used:

  • Diamond polishing: What it is: Progressively finer diamonds refine the terrazzo itself; Trade-offs: Generally the longest-lasting shine; more machine time
  • Polishing powders: What it is: Ground and honed, then polished with powders that use acids; Trade-offs: Quick shine; usually needs re-buffing every couple of years
  • Recrystallization: What it is: Honed, then an acid-based treatment on the top surface; Trade-offs: Glossy result; wears and needs regular re-treatment
  • Topical coating or wax: What it is: A film applied on top; Trade-offs: Looks shiny at first; scratches, yellows, builds up and must be stripped

Many restorers combine methods. What matters is knowing what you're paying for and what maintenance it will need.

What patches will look like

Good restorers match chip color, size and binder shade closely, and small repairs like tack holes and chips usually disappear once ground and polished. Be realistic about larger areas:

  • Where walls were moved, there may be a strip with no terrazzo, or a different pattern.
  • Plumbing and electrical cuts can show as a slightly different tone.
  • Divider strip layouts may not continue perfectly through a patch.
  • Ask to see a finished repair in a corner before the whole floor is done.

Terrazzo in condos

Many older Miami Beach and Miami condo buildings have terrazzo under carpet. Before you pull it up in a unit with another unit below:

  • Read your association's flooring rules. Some require carpet or a sound-rated floor in upper units, and exposing a hard floor may not be allowed.
  • Check with the building department if any new flooring or underlayment is involved; Miami Beach, for example, requires flooring permits with soundproofing documentation.
  • Get approval in writing before removing carpet.

  • Colonial Stone and Floor Care (Miami): Published price per sq ft: $5–$20; Scope: Overall restoration range
  • Tampa restoration contractor: Published price per sq ft: $5–$12; Scope: Condition, size and repairs

What moves the price most: adhesive and old coatings, the number of repairs, lippage, the finish level, and access (an empty open floor costs less per foot than a furnished home with many small rooms).

Restoring sound terrazzo is usually far cheaper than installing a new floor of similar quality.

Caring for restored terrazzo

  • Dust-mop daily in sandy areas; grit scratches the surface.
  • Use a pH-neutral stone cleaner. Avoid vinegar, citrus, bleach and ammonia, which etch marble chips and the cement binder.
  • Reseal with a penetrating sealer as recommended.
  • Skip wax and topical coatings, which build up and dull the floor.
  • Mats at entrances, especially near the beach.

Common questions

How much does terrazzo restoration cost in Miami?

Published Florida prices mostly run from about $3 to $8 per sq ft for grinding, honing and polishing a sound floor, and $5 to $20 or more once glue removal and repairs are involved. Rebuilding badly damaged terrazzo can reach $20 to $50 per sq ft.

Is it worth restoring terrazzo?

Usually. Original Miami terrazzo is durable, hard to replicate and often cheaper to restore than to replace with a floor of similar quality. Restoration only stops making sense where large areas are missing or badly damaged.

Can you remove carpet glue and tack-strip holes from terrazzo?

Yes. Adhesive is removed before grinding, and tack-strip holes, chips and cracks are filled with color-matched material and ground flush. Hundreds of holes take time, which is why adhesive and repairs move the price most.

Is there asbestos under my old vinyl tile?

Possibly. Black adhesive under vinyl tile laid before the mid-1980s can contain asbestos. Have it lab-tested before anyone grinds; carpet laid straight over terrazzo usually doesn't carry that risk.

Can I uncover terrazzo in my condo?

Check your association's flooring rules first. Some buildings require carpet or a sound-rated floor in units above others, and taking up carpet to expose a hard floor can conflict with those rules.

Will patches show?

Good color-matched repairs blend well, but large patches, plumbing cuts and places where walls were moved can still be visible up close. Ask to see a repaired area before the whole floor is done.

Diamond polishing or crystallization?

Diamond polishing refines the terrazzo itself and generally lasts longer. Polishing powders and crystallization can produce a quick shine but usually need re-buffing every couple of years. Ask which method a quote uses.

How do I look after terrazzo?

Dust-mop to remove sand, clean with a pH-neutral stone cleaner, avoid vinegar and acidic or bleach cleaners, and reseal with a penetrating sealer as recommended. Avoid building up wax or topical coatings.
