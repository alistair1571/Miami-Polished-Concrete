Polished concrete, epoxy or something else?

Get this decision right before anything else. Polished concrete is the best-value floor for most commercial spaces, but not all of them.

Your spaceBest fitWhy
Dry storage warehouse, distributionPolished concreteNo coating to wear through under forklifts. Dust-proofs the slab.
Retail, showroom, office, lobbyPolished concreteLooks premium, reflects light, no waxing.
Restaurant front of house, barPolished concrete with slip-resistant guardDurable and cleanable. Needs wet-slip planning.
Commercial kitchen, brewery productionUrethane cementHandles hot water, grease, thermal shock and constant wet.
Battery charging, chemical storage, labsEpoxy or chemical-resistant coatingNeeds a sealed, chemically resistant barrier.
Cold storage and freezersPolished or densified concrete, or a low-temperature resinStandard epoxy becomes brittle in the cold.
Damaged, uneven or patched slabPolished overlay or microcementA new wearing surface gives a consistent look.

Why polishing wins in Miami: a coating is a film on top of the slab. Miami slabs sit close to a high water table and carry moisture, especially during the May to October wet season. Moisture vapor that builds up under a coating is one of the main reasons epoxy blisters and peels in South Florida. A polished floor has no film, so vapor passes through instead of lifting anything.

Polished concrete by industry

Warehouses, distribution and logistics

Common across Doral, Medley, Hialeah, Miami Lakes and the airport cargo corridor. What matters:

  • Dust-proofing. Unsealed slabs shed fine concrete dust onto stock and racking. Densifying stops it.
  • Joint protection. Forklift wheels break down control joint edges. Fill joints with a semi-rigid polyurea, and not too early on a new slab, or the filler separates as the slab shrinks.
  • Racking. Floors are usually polished in open aisles and staging areas without moving racking.
  • Flatness. Polishing refines the surface but doesn't fix a slab that's out of level. If you run very narrow aisle or turret trucks, ask about flatness surveys and corrective grinding.
  • Finish level. Most warehouses finish at matte to satin. Higher gloss isn't needed and adds cost.

Cold storage and freezers

Polished or densified concrete handles forklift traffic in coolers and freezers with nothing to crack or delaminate in the cold. Work is planned around temperature: some systems need the room brought up to temperature, while fast-curing resins like MMA can go down cold. Plan the schedule with your stock rotation.

Retail stores and showrooms

Miracle Mile, the Design District, Brickell, Dadeland, Aventura and every furniture, tile and car showroom along the major corridors. A semi-gloss or high-gloss polish reflects display lighting and makes a space look finished without the cost and grout maintenance of large-format tile. Dyes, saw-cut grids and brand-color accents can be built in.

Auto dealerships and service bays

Showroom floors take a high-gloss polish. Service bays need oil and fluid resistance, so ask about a stronger guard or a coating in the work zones.

Restaurants, bars and cafés

Polished concrete works well for dining rooms, bars and host stands. Plan for wet slip resistance at entrances, bar lines and service stations with an anti-slip guard and mats. Kitchens and dish areas should be urethane cement, not polished concrete.

Hotels, offices, healthcare and schools

High-gloss or full-aggregate finishes give lobbies a terrazzo-like look at a lower cost, phased overnight around guests. Offices swap carpet tile for polish, with rugs and ceiling treatments for acoustics. Clinics get a seamless, easy-to-disinfect floor; confirm your disinfectants are compatible with the guard. Schools and churches lose the waxing budget every summer.

Parking garages

Covered, dry parking decks can be polished or densified to cut dust and make cleaning easier. Exposed ramps and uncovered decks get slippery when wet, so they need a traction finish instead.

Doing the work without closing your business

  • Phasing. The floor is split into zones. Each is completed and handed back before the next starts.
  • Night and weekend work. Common for retail, restaurants and offices. The floor can take foot traffic by the next morning.
  • Return to service. Foot traffic is typically possible straight after the final pass. Pallet jacks and forklifts return once the guard and joint filler have cured, often within a day. Confirm it in the proposal.
  • Dust. Grinders run with HEPA vacuum extraction and must meet the OSHA respirable crystalline silica standard. Ask for the contractor's silica exposure control plan.
  • Power. Large grinders often need three-phase power. Where it isn't available, propane grinders are used, which need ventilation planning in occupied buildings.

Slip resistance, safety and compliance

  • Slip resistance. Dry, polished concrete is generally comparable to other hard floors. Wet, it can become slippery. Ask your contractor to test to ANSI A326.3; a wet DCOF of 0.42 or higher is the benchmark for level interior floors expected to get wet.
  • Accessibility. The ADA Standards call for floors on accessible routes to be firm, stable and slip resistant. A seamless floor with no raised thresholds or tile lips helps meet that.
  • Food service. Food prep areas need smooth, durable, nonabsorbent, easily cleanable surfaces. Dining rooms can be polished; kitchens need a system built for wet, hot and greasy conditions.
  • Silica. Grinding concrete creates respirable silica dust. OSHA requires controls on grinders.
  • Asbestos in older buildings. Old 9x9 tile, some VCT and black mastic can contain asbestos. For commercial renovation, federal rules generally require an inspection before materials are disturbed. See the asbestos guide.

Specifying polished concrete

Polished concrete is only as good as its spec. Vague specs ("polish to high gloss") are how owners end up with grind and seal. Write these into the scope, typically under CSI MasterFormat section 03 35 43, Polished Concrete Finishing:

  • Aggregate exposure class: cream, fine (salt and pepper), medium or large
  • Gloss level: with a target reading measured by gloss or DOI meter
  • Finish grit and sequence, including the final resin grit
  • Densifier type and application point
  • Guard, and whether it's burnished in
  • Joint treatment: filler type, and timing relative to slab age
  • Crack repair method with color-match requirements
  • Mock-up: an approved sample area that becomes the acceptance standard
  • Slip resistance: ANSI A326.3 wet DCOF target and testing
  • Dust control and protection after completion

New slabs and ground-up builds

The best commercial polished floors are planned before the pour: a consistent mix and aggregate, a hard-trowel finish with flatness targets, no curing compounds that block densifiers, a planned joint layout, and protection from other trades until polishing. A contractor can review your concrete and finishing specs at design stage. More on new slabs.

Tenant improvements and space turnovers

For landlords and property managers, polished concrete is a floor that survives tenant turnover. Between leases it usually needs a clean and burnish, not replacement.

  • Existing VCT, carpet tile, LVT and adhesives are removed and the slab is ground clean
  • Old wall lines, trenches and patches will show. A dye, darker finish or overlay makes the floor uniform where the look matters.
  • Schedule polishing after demo and MEP but before millwork, fixtures and final paint
  • Get an asbestos survey before removing old flooring in older buildings

How to compare commercial polishing bids

Two bids for "polished concrete" can describe very different floors. Check each one covers:

  1. Polish method: true mechanical polish, or grind and seal
  2. Aggregate class and target gloss level, measured
  3. Final grit
  4. Densifier and guard products
  5. Removal of existing flooring and disposal
  6. Crack, spall and joint work, by linear foot
  7. Mock-up area and approval process
  8. Phasing, working hours and premiums
  9. Dust control and silica plan
  10. Protection after completion
  11. Certificate of insurance, with your company as additional insured if required
  12. Warranty terms and exclusions
  13. Maintenance guidance

What to send for an accurate proposal: square footage or a floor plan, photos of the floor, existing flooring type, working hours, racking and equipment layout, power availability, and your deadline.

Common questions

Can you polish our floor without closing the business?

Usually. Work is phased by zone and done at night or on weekends. Foot traffic can typically return the next morning.

How soon can forklifts go back on the floor?

It depends on the guard and joint filler used, often within a day of the final pass. Your contractor will confirm it in the proposal.

Polished concrete or epoxy for a warehouse?

For dry storage and distribution, polished concrete usually wins because there's no coating to wear through, peel or recoat. Epoxy is the better choice for chemical exposure, battery charging areas or where you need a fully sealed surface.

Is polished concrete slippery for customers and staff?

Dry, it's generally comparable to other hard floors. Wet, it can be slippery. Test to ANSI A326.3 and use an anti-slip guard and mats in wet areas and entrances.

What about our control joints?

They stay, and they need protection. Filling them with a semi-rigid polyurea supports the edges against forklift and cart traffic.

Can you polish around racking?

Yes. Aisles and open areas are typically polished without moving racking. Polishing under racking is done in phases if needed.

Can you polish over VCT or carpet tile?

The existing flooring and adhesive are removed first. In older buildings, have the flooring and mastic tested for asbestos before removal.

Does polished concrete help with LEED?

It can contribute, because it uses the existing slab, needs no coatings or waxes and uses low-VOC products. Confirm the specific credits with your LEED consultant.

How long does a commercial polished floor last?

The polish itself lasts for decades. High-traffic areas need periodic burnishing and occasional re-polishing to keep their gloss.

What does commercial polished concrete cost per square foot in Miami?

Typically $3 to $6 per sq ft on large floors over 5,000 sq ft, and $5 to $12 on small retail and office floors. Removal, repairs and night work add cost.

Do you provide certificates of insurance and safety documents?

Your contractor can provide a COI, including additional insured status where required, and their safety and silica documentation.

Do you work with GCs on new builds?

Yes. The best results come from planning the polish before the pour, so involve a contractor at the design or pre-bid stage.
